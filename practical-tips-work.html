Practical Tips — At Work

For years I was the person with brilliant ideas who couldn't file a report on time. These are the things that have actually closed that gap.

← All Practical Tips As an Adult As a Parent At Work Through a Child's Eyes

Most workplaces are built around sustained, linear attention — long meetings, open-plan interruptions, vague verbal instructions you're expected to just remember. None of that plays to an ADHD brain's strengths. These are the adjustments — some tiny, some requiring a slightly awkward conversation — that have made the biggest difference for me.

  • Treat your inbox as an in-tray, not a to-do list. Emails are not tasks — they're requests to eventually turn into tasks. If a message needs action, pull it into your actual task system straight away. Otherwise it just sits there quietly generating background guilt.
  • Write down what you want to say before you forget it. In meetings, the thought you had thirty seconds ago has genuinely gone by the time there's a gap to speak. Jot a one-word trigger the moment it lands — it's usually enough to bring the whole thought back when your turn comes.
  • Block real focus time, and defend it. An empty calendar slot gets filled by other people's priorities within a day. Put recurring "focus time" in your calendar as if it were a real meeting, and decline things that land on it unless they genuinely can't move.
  • Turn vague instructions into a checklist immediately. "Can you sort the client report" is not something your memory can hold reliably for three days. The moment a task lands — verbally or in writing — convert it into concrete written steps. Future you will have no idea what past you meant otherwise.
  • Set yourself an earlier, fake deadline. Real deadlines don't feel real until they're dangerously close — that's just how ADHD urgency perception works. Give yourself an artificial deadline a few days early, and treat it as seriously as the real one.
  • Keep a "parking lot" for stray ideas. A brilliant unrelated idea mid-task is a genuine hazard — it can hijack the next two hours if you let it. Write it in one line in a running note and get straight back to what you were doing. It'll still be there later.
  • Batch similar tasks together. Switching between different types of work — admin, calls, deep work, email — carries a real cognitive cost. Group similar tasks into blocks rather than interleaving them through the day.
  • Consider disclosing, on your own terms. You don't owe anyone your diagnosis. But a good manager, told clearly what actually helps you (written instructions, agenda-in-advance, fewer last-minute changes), can make reasonable adjustments that make a genuinely outsized difference. Decide deliberately rather than by default.
